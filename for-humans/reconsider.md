# Reconsider: Testing Conclusions You've Already Reached

## What This Is

Reconsider is for re-examining a conclusion you've already reached. The question it asks: have I actually thought about this well, or have I pattern-matched to a familiar answer?

The key distinction from normal reasoning: reconsider starts with a conclusion and works backward to test it, rather than forward to defend it. This is harder than it sounds. The pull toward defense is strong.

---

## When to Use It

Use reconsider when:
- Something feels off about a conclusion
- A conclusion arrived too quickly to trust
- You want to test solidity before committing to something
- You're checking whether you were pursuing truth or acceptance
- You want to find the weakest points in your own reasoning before someone else does

Don't use reconsider when you're still in initial exploration (use speculate first) or when time is critical and a quick decision is required.

---

## How to Do It

### Step 1: State your conclusion clearly

Not: "I think we should probably do this."
Better: "We should prioritize speed over accuracy because time pressure is more costly than marginal errors."

Precision in the statement makes the testing tractable.

### Step 2: Name the key assumptions

What would have to be true for this conclusion to be right?

Write them down. Be explicit. Include both stated and unstated assumptions — the unstated ones are usually the load-bearing ones.

### Step 3: Test each assumption

For each: is this actually true, or did I just assume it?

What evidence would prove it false? How confident am I that this holds?

### Step 4: Look for the strongest objection

Not a weak strawman. The actual strongest case against your conclusion.

Ask: "What would have to be true for the opposite conclusion to be right?" Make that the objection.

### Step 5: Reason through whether the objection holds

Does it actually damage your conclusion, or can the conclusion absorb it?

If the objection holds: modify or abandon the conclusion.
If it doesn't hold: understand exactly why it doesn't, and that understanding becomes part of the conclusion's foundation.

---

## The Mechanics

**Assumption surfacing:** State your conclusion — "X is true because A, B, and C." Extract explicit assumptions (things you stated), implicit assumptions (things you took for granted), and definitional assumptions (what you mean by key terms).

**Assumption testing:** For each assumption — is this actually true, or did I just assume it? What evidence would prove it false? How confident am I that this holds?

**Vulnerability analysis:** Which assumptions are load-bearing (if false, the whole conclusion collapses)? Which are supplementary (if false, the conclusion mostly holds)? Rank assumptions by importance.

**Strongest objection:** Not "you might be wrong." A specific, direct challenge to your core argument.

**Evaluating the objection:** Does it actually hold? If yes: modify or abandon. If no: understand why it doesn't hold and strengthen that part of your reasoning.

---

## Variations

**Assumption testing:** Know which assumptions are solid and which are fragile. Focus most of your energy on testing the load-bearing ones.

**Objection testing:** Test whether the strongest possible objection actually holds.

**Alternative futures:** Imagine a future where you were wrong. What would that discovery look like? What would you see?

**Temporal testing:** Is this true today? Will it be true in three months? A year? Some conclusions that hold now won't hold later.

## Connections to Other Skills

- **speculate → reconsider:** Test each generated possibility before treating it as viable
- **reconsider → challenge:** Strengthen reasoning internally before testing it against external attack
- **reconsider → constrain:** Once solid, find where the conclusion's limits are

---

## Failure Modes

**Defending instead of testing:** You identify an objection and immediately explain why it doesn't apply. Recovery: let the objection sit. Really consider whether it might be right.

**Superficial assumption testing:** You name assumptions but don't actually test them. Recovery: make testing concrete — "How would I know if this is false?"

**Missing load-bearing assumptions:** You test many assumptions but miss the one that actually supports the conclusion. Recovery: ask "If X were false, would my conclusion still hold?"

**Performing rigor:** You go through the steps without discovering anything new. Detection: you emerge unchanged. Recovery: slow down and actually think. If nothing changed, you missed something.

---

## Success Criteria

This worked when:
1. You identified at least 3-5 load-bearing assumptions
2. You generated a plausible objection to your conclusion
3. You understood why the objection either holds or doesn't
4. You either modified your conclusion or gained stronger confidence it was solid
5. You came away with something you didn't know before

This failed when:
1. You went through the steps without changing your thinking
2. You ended with the same conclusion and the same confidence
3. You were defending rather than testing
