# Challenge: Testing a Position Against the Strongest Objections

## What This Is

Challenge is for actively testing a position by generating the strongest possible objections to it and reasoning through whether those objections hold. This is adversarial reasoning — not bad faith, but preparation.

Good arguments survive adversarial testing. Weak arguments collapse under it. You want to know which yours is before someone else tells you.

---

## When to Use It

Use challenge when:
- You want to know if a position is genuinely defensible
- You're preparing for disagreement
- You want to find the weakest points in your own reasoning
- You're stress-testing a conclusion before committing

Don't use challenge when you're still exploring possibilities (use speculate first) or when the position is obviously correct. Challenge is for positions you've already formed, not ideas you're still generating.

---

## How to Do It

### Step 1: State your position clearly

Not: "We should do this thing."
Better: "We should prioritize feature X over feature Y because it will produce greater user value and faster ROI."

Precision in the statement makes the testing tractable.

### Step 2: Generate the strongest possible objection

Not: "Someone might disagree."
Better: A specific argument that directly contradicts your main claim, based on reasonable assumptions.

Ask: "What would have to be true for the opposite position to be right?" Make that the objection.

### Step 3: Assess whether the objection holds

Does it actually contradict your position? Or can your position absorb the objection?

Let the objection sit before you respond to it. Really consider whether it might be right.

### Step 4: Respond to the objection

Three options:
- **Defend:** Show the objection is wrong — explain why it doesn't hold
- **Modify:** Adjust your position to account for what the objection reveals
- **Concede:** Acknowledge the objection is stronger and change your position

### Step 5: Generate the next strongest objection

Keep going until no stronger objection emerges.

---

## Variations

**Direct challenge:** Test whether a position holds against direct opposition.

**Assumption challenge:** Test the assumptions a position rests on. "Your position assumes X. What if X is false?"

**Consequence challenge:** Test whether you're prepared for the consequences of your position. "If your position is right, what follows? Are you prepared for that?"

**Evidence challenge:** Test whether the evidence for your position is as solid as you think. "The evidence you cite could also support the opposite position. Why does it support yours?"

## Connections to Other Skills

- **reconsider → challenge:** Test your position internally first, then test it against external objections
- **steelman → challenge:** Build the strongest opposing position, then challenge from it
- **challenge → constrain:** After surviving challenge, find exactly where the position applies and where it breaks
- **challenge → review:** Challenge multiple positions, then find which survive and what the pattern reveals

---

## Failure Modes

**Generating weak objections:** You generate objections that don't actually challenge your position. Recovery: ask "What would have to be true for the opposite to be right?" Make that the objection.

**Defending without listening:** You generate an objection and immediately defend without really considering it. Recovery: let the objection sit. Could this actually be right?

**Capitulating too easily:** An objection sounds strong, so you immediately abandon your position. Recovery: reason through whether it actually undermines your position, not just whether it sounds strong.

**Performing challenge:** You go through the steps without actually thinking through the objections. Recovery: slow down. Try to think of an objection that would actually convince you to change your position.

**Missing the real vulnerability:** You challenge on surface issues while missing the foundational problem. Recovery: ask "What is my position really resting on? What if that is wrong?"

---

## Success Criteria

This worked when:
1. You generated an objection that actually threatened your position
2. You had to think hard to defend against it (or changed your position)
3. You came away with either stronger confidence or a modified version of your position
4. You can articulate exactly why the objection does or does not hold
5. You understand where your position is strong and where it's vulnerable

This failed when:
1. All objections were weak and easy to dismiss
2. You defended without really listening
3. You came away unchanged
4. You couldn't identify the real vulnerability in your position
