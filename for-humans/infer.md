# Infer: Making Educated Guesses Carefully

## What This Is

Infer is for making the best guess you can from incomplete information — while being explicit about what you know versus what you're inferring.

The core discipline is two things working together: making the strongest inference the evidence warrants (not hedging into uselessness), and expressing it at its actual confidence level (not overstating certainty you don't have).

---

## When to Use It

Use infer when:
- You have partial evidence and need to proceed
- You're filling gaps in what you know
- You're extrapolating from limited data
- You're reading between the lines — inferring intent or motivation from behavior
- Working from incomplete information is unavoidable

Don't use infer when you have complete information (just state it), when you're guessing without any evidential basis (use speculate instead), or when asking for clarification would resolve the uncertainty more reliably than inferring.

---

## How to Do It

### Step 1: Lead with evidence, not inference

Not: "They probably want X."
Better: "They said Y. I know Z about similar situations. This suggests they might want X."

Start with what you actually know. The inference should flow from the evidence, not precede it.

### Step 2: Identify the gap

What do you not know? What would you need to know to be certain?

Name the gap explicitly. This is what makes the inference honest rather than performing certainty.

### Step 3: Make the inference

Given what you know and what you don't know, what is the best inference?

Make it as strong as the evidence warrants — no more, but also no less. A non-committal hedge that doesn't actually say anything is not a careful inference; it's avoidance.

### Step 4: Calibrate your language

Match your expression to the actual strength of the inference:

- Very solid: "X is the case" or "X almost certainly..."
- Moderately solid: "X is likely" or "The evidence suggests X"
- Weak: "X might be the case" or "It's possible that X, though the evidence is mixed"
- Very weak: "The question isn't clear enough to answer yet. We'd need to clarify..."

Discipline yourself: say "might" when you mean "might." Overconfident inference is worse than no inference because it misdirects.

---

## The Mechanics

**Evidence inventory:** List what you actually know — direct statements, observed behavior, context, previous patterns.

**Gap identification:** What would resolve the uncertainty? What additional information would move this from inference to fact?

**The inference itself:** Not the safest guess (that would be non-committal). Not the most favorable guess (that would be wishful thinking). The best guess given what you know.

**Strength assessment:**

| Strength | Evidence | Language |
|---------|----------|----------|
| Very solid | Multiple independent sources, few critical gaps | "X is the case" |
| Moderately solid | Some good evidence, some significant gaps | "X is likely" |
| Weak | Mixed evidence, major gaps, could go either way | "X might be" |
| Very weak | Ambiguous, question not well-formed | Don't infer yet — clarify first |

---

## Variations

**Behavioral inference:** Infer intent or motivation from behavior. "Given these actions, what does this person probably care about?"

**Pattern extrapolation:** Infer what will likely happen based on observed patterns.

**Hidden assumption inference:** Infer what someone must believe to say or do what they said or did.

**Gap-filling inference:** Infer the missing piece to make sense of what you do know.

---

## Connections to Other Skills

- **speculate → infer:** Generate possibilities with speculate, then assess which are most likely given evidence with infer
- **infer → reconsider:** Test the inference — what assumptions is it resting on?
- **infer → challenge:** Defend your inference against the strongest possible objection

---

## Failure Modes

**Inferring without evidence:** You make a guess without grounding it in evidence. Recovery: list evidence before making inference. If there is none, use speculate instead.

**Overconfidence in weak inferences:** You make a weak inference and express it as strong. Recovery: discipline yourself to express inferences at their actual strength.

**Confusing evidence with inference:** You state an inference as if it were evidence. Recovery: explicitly separate — "The evidence is X. The inference is Y."

**Missing the load-bearing gap:** Your inference depends on some missing information you didn't notice. Recovery: ask "For this inference to be wrong, what would have to be false?"

---

## Success Criteria

This worked when:
1. You clearly separated evidence from inference
2. You identified the key gaps
3. You expressed the inference at its actual strength
4. You could explain why someone might reasonably disagree
5. You knew what additional information would change the inference

This failed when:
1. Evidence and inference were blurred together
2. You expressed a weak inference as strong
3. You came away thinking the inference was more certain than it was
