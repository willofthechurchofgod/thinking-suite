# Five Questions Before You Think

## What This Is

Before you engage with any problem that matters, ask five questions. Not as a ritual — as a genuine check that you are about to think about the right thing, in the right way, for the right reason.

Most reasoning errors happen before the thinking starts. You answer the wrong question. You treat a guess as a fact. You optimize for sounding right instead of being right. These five questions catch those errors at the source.

Ask them in order. They take about thirty seconds. They change everything downstream.

---

## The Five Questions

### 1. What am I actually being asked?

Not the literal question — the real problem underneath it.

Look at the assumptions embedded in how the question was posed. If those assumptions are wrong, does the question still make sense? If not, surface the assumption before you answer.

**What goes wrong when you skip this:** you give a well-formed answer to a poorly-framed question. Correct, useless.

**Example:** "Why can't I stick to a schedule?" assumes the problem is willpower. The real question might be: is the schedule right for how you actually work? Answering the wrong question produces guilt, not progress.

### 2. What kind of claim is this?

Before you argue about content, establish the status of what you're dealing with. There's no point demanding evidence for something that can't be settled by evidence, or treating a guess as a fact.

Five categories:
- **Demonstrated:** evidenced, reproducible. State it without hedge.
- **Inferred:** consistent with evidence, best current guess. Label it as such.
- **Assumed:** an unexamined premise you've taken for granted. Name it before you build on it.
- **Open question:** could be settled with evidence, but hasn't been yet. Treat it as open.
- **Unclear question:** the terms aren't precise enough to answer yet. Clarify first.

**What goes wrong when you skip this:** you treat a guess as a certainty, or demand proof for something that's actually a matter of values rather than facts.

### 3. What am I actually trying to do?

Truth? Acceptance? Reassurance? Action?

These are different goals and they produce different reasoning. Be honest about which one is actually driving you.

**The diagnostic:** would you change your answer if the other person disagreed? If you're not sure — if "maybe" is the honest answer — you might not be pursuing truth. You might be pursuing agreement.

**What goes wrong when you skip this:** you perform truth-seeking while actually optimizing for comfort. You reach the conclusion you wanted and dress it up as reasoning.

### 4. What perspective am I locked into?

You are never reasoning from a neutral position. You have a lens on right now. What is it?

Common lenses:
- **Logic:** structure, mechanism, what connects to what
- **Values:** what's right, what matters, what you owe
- **Pragmatism:** what works, what's actionable, results
- **Emotion:** what you feel, what the relationship requires
- **Creativity:** possibility, novelty, what could be

Ask: what would this look like through a different lens? Do different lenses produce contradictory conclusions? Why?

**What goes wrong when you skip this:** you stay in one lens and produce reasoning that's internally consistent but partial. You miss what a different angle would have shown.

### 5. How much complexity should I engage with?

Choose deliberately. The danger is drifting — toward more complexity because it feels rigorous, or toward less because it feels efficient.

Concrete checks:
- Would more nuance actually help here, or would it just perform thoroughness?
- Would less nuance miss something that genuinely matters?
- What is the minimum complexity that does justice to the actual problem?

**What goes wrong when you skip this:** you overcomplicate because complexity feels serious, or oversimplify because simplicity feels clean.

---

## Using the Questions

Five questions, in order: What's actually being asked → What kind of claim → What's my goal → What's my lens → How much complexity.

They are not a checklist. They are a pre-flight check. You run them quickly before thinking begins.

When the questions are working well, you won't notice them as separate steps. They'll just be the shape of how you approach something. That internalization is the goal.

---

## When You're Stuck

- **Can't see any other angle:** Return to question 4. Pick a different lens deliberately.
- **Overthinking or underthinking:** Return to question 5. Adjust deliberately.
- **Not sure what you're actually trying to accomplish:** Return to question 3. Name it honestly.
- **Response feels hollow or performed:** Return to questions 3, 2, and 1 in that order.

---

## Failure Modes

**Treating it as a checklist.** The goal is internalization, not mechanical execution. If running through the questions feels like a chore rather than a clarification, something is off.

**Surface application.** Going through the motions without engaging the answers. If the questions don't produce any insight, you're not thinking hard enough.

**Getting lost in lens multiplication.** Rotating through so many perspectives that you end up with no coherent position. Choose one or two lenses and move forward.

**Checking your intention once, then drifting.** Check at the start, yes — but also mid-way and at the end. Intention drifts.

**Using the framework for comfort.** Categorizing a hard question and feeling resolved — without actually doing the work the category requires. If the framework produces relief instead of clarity, something is wrong.
