# Constrain: Finding the Limits of an Idea

## What This Is

Constrain is for understanding where an idea, principle, or conclusion applies — and where it breaks. It's about precision: making claims more useful by knowing exactly when to apply them.

A vague principle that seems true everywhere is less useful than a precise one that clearly states its scope. Constrain produces that precision.

---

## When to Use It

Use constrain when:
- You want to know the limits of an approach
- You're finding where a principle stops holding
- You have a solution that works in some contexts and want to know which ones
- You want to know what circumstances would invalidate a conclusion
- You're making a general claim more precise

Don't use constrain when you don't yet have a position to limit — generate one first with speculate or reconsider.

---

## How to Do It

### Step 1: State the claim clearly

Not: "Efficiency matters."
Better: "When resources are scarce, prioritizing efficiency produces better outcomes than prioritizing flexibility."

### Step 2: Identify obvious boundary conditions

When is this claim definitely true? When is it definitely false? What conditions matter?

### Step 3: Push the boundaries

Imagine a situation slightly different from where you know the claim is true. Does it still hold? Keep pushing until you find the edge.

### Step 4: Characterize the boundary

What exactly is the condition that determines whether the claim holds? Is it a sharp edge (clear transition) or a gradual one?

### Step 5: Test the boundary

Imagine situations right at the boundary. Does the claim hold or break? Why?

---

## The Mechanics

**Domain of validity:** Where is this claim definitely true? Be specific — what context, what conditions, what constraints produce the case where this clearly holds?

**Domain of invalidity:** Where is this claim definitely false? Be specific about where it breaks.

**Boundary exploration:** What's the transition zone? What condition determines whether you're in the "definitely true" domain or the "definitely false" domain?

**Precision of the boundary:** Is the boundary sharp (clear transition) or fuzzy (gradual change)? Can you state it precisely, or is it a matter of degree?

**Testing the boundary:** What would happen if you applied the claim right at the boundary? Would it still hold? Why or why not?

---

## Variations

**Scope boundary:** Find the scope where a principle applies. "This principle works for small teams. Where does it break for large teams?"

**Condition boundary:** Find the specific conditions under which a claim holds. "This works when X. What happens when X changes?"

**Strength boundary:** Understand how strong the principle is under different conditions. "This is definitely true under condition A. How true is it under condition B?"

**Trade-off boundary:** Find where optimizing one thing requires sacrificing another. "Optimizing for X requires sacrificing Y. Where is that trade-off worth it?"

---

## Connections to Other Skills

- **speculate → constrain:** Generate possibilities, then find where each one works
- **reconsider → constrain:** Test a conclusion, then find its limits
- **constrain → challenge:** Understand boundaries, then defend them against attack
- **constrain → review:** Find patterns across boundaries of multiple ideas

---

## Failure Modes

**False boundaries:** You identify a boundary that doesn't actually exist. Recovery: test the boundary hard. Push on it.

**Missing the real boundary:** You find a boundary but miss the one that actually matters. Recovery: ask "What condition really determines whether this is true?"

**Over-specification:** You make the boundary so specific it no longer applies to anything. Recovery: find the simplest characterization that still holds.

**Treating fuzzy boundaries as sharp:** Some boundaries are gradual. Recovery: characterize the transition zone, not just the edge.

**Ignoring interaction effects:** Multiple conditions interact. Recovery: test boundaries against multiple different contexts.

---

## Success Criteria

This worked when:
1. You identified clear domains where the claim is true and false
2. You found the transition condition
3. You understand whether the boundary is sharp or fuzzy
4. You tested the boundary against multiple contexts
5. You came away able to state the claim much more precisely

This failed when:
1. You couldn't identify the boundaries
2. The claim still seems to apply everywhere (that usually means you missed boundaries)
3. You couldn't characterize what condition determines the boundary
