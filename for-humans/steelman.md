# Steelman: Building the Strongest Version of a Position You Disagree With

## What This Is

Steelman is for genuinely understanding a position before engaging with it. Not the weakest version you can knock down — the strongest version a thoughtful defender would actually hold.

It runs in two stages because there are two distinct ways a steelman can fail: you can win the logical argument while completely missing why the position matters to the person holding it. Both stages are required for genuine understanding.

Steelman precedes challenge. You can't effectively challenge a position you haven't genuinely understood.

---

## When to Use It

Use steelman when:
- You're preparing to argue against something
- You notice yourself dismissing a position too quickly
- Someone holds a view that seems wrong or frustrating
- You want to engage seriously rather than score points

Don't use steelman when you already agree with the position (that's just understanding, which is good, but not what this skill is for), when you need to evaluate evidence (use infer or reconsider), or when the goal is simply to defeat the argument without genuinely engaging (use challenge, but recognize you'll be engaging with a weaker version than you could).

---

## Stage 1: Logical Steelman

### What it produces

The strongest rational case for the position. The evidence, reasoning, and values a thoughtful defender would actually cite — not a caricature, not a strawman, the real argument at full strength.

### How to do it

**Step 1: Suspend your objections**
Not permanently. Temporarily. You can't reconstruct a position you're simultaneously arguing against. Set your objections aside for the duration of Stage 1.

**Step 2: Identify the core claim**
What is the position actually asserting? State it precisely, in terms its defender would recognize. Not your paraphrase — their claim.

**Step 3: Find the strongest evidence**
What evidence would a thoughtful defender cite? Not cherry-picked or weak evidence — the best available evidence that genuinely supports the position.

**Step 4: Find the strongest reasoning**
Given that evidence, what is the most coherent logical path to the conclusion? What assumptions does the position rest on, and are those assumptions defensible?

**Step 5: Find the values underneath**
Every position rests on values, not just facts. What values — prioritized in what order — would lead a reasonable person to this conclusion? Name them explicitly. This is where many steelmans fail: they reconstruct the logic but miss the value structure underneath it.

**Step 6: State the steelman**
Articulate the position as its strongest defender would. If you showed this to a thoughtful proponent, would they say "yes, that's my argument, stated better than I stated it"?

That is the test. If the answer is no, you haven't completed Stage 1 yet.

---

## Stage 2: Empathic Steelman

### What it produces

Understanding of why a reasonable person holds this position genuinely — what experience, context, or concern makes it feel not just defensible but important.

### Why this stage is necessary

You can complete Stage 1 and still be condescending. Logical steelman reconstructs the argument. Empathic steelman reconstructs the person making it. Both are required for genuine engagement.

Winning an argument while completely missing why it was being made is one of the most common and least noticed failures in reasoning.

### How to do it

**Step 1: Ask what the position is responding to**
Every position is a response to something — an experience, a fear, an observation, a pattern someone has seen. What is this position responding to? Not abstractly. Concretely. What would someone have had to see or experience to arrive here?

**Step 2: Find the legitimate concern**
Underneath most positions that seem wrong is a legitimate concern that the position is (perhaps poorly) trying to address. What is the legitimate concern here? Name it without sarcasm. If you can't name it without sarcasm, you haven't found it yet.

**Step 3: Understand the stakes**
Why does this matter to the person holding it? What's at stake for them if they're wrong, or if the opposite position wins? Stakes explain intensity. A position held with unusual conviction is usually protecting something real.

**Step 4: Reconstruct the lived logic**
Given the concern, the stakes, and the experience that produced this view — what is the internal logic that makes this position feel not just acceptable but necessary? Not "why are they wrong" — why does it feel right from inside.

**Step 5: State the empathic steelman**
Articulate what the position means to its holder. If you showed this to someone who holds the view, would they say "yes, you actually understand why I believe this"?

That is the test. Not agreement — understanding.

---

## The Integration

Stage 1 and Stage 2 are sequential, not parallel.

Logical steelman first — because you need to understand what is being claimed before you can understand why it's held.

Empathic steelman second — because understanding why it's held often reveals dimensions of the claim that the logical reconstruction missed.

After both stages: you have a complete steelman. You understand the argument at its strongest and the person making it at their most genuine.

Only now should you engage with challenge, reconsider, or constrain.

---

## Connections to Other Skills

**steelman → challenge:** You can't effectively challenge a position you haven't genuinely understood. Run steelman first, then challenge the strongest version.

**steelman → review:** When you've steelmanned multiple competing positions, use review to find what each position is seeing that the others miss.

**steelman → constrain:** Once you understand a position at full strength, use constrain to find where it applies and where it breaks.

## Failure Modes

**The logical-only steelman:** You complete Stage 1 but skip Stage 2. You have the best argument but not the person. You'll engage with the argument and miss the conversation.
Recovery: Stage 2 is not optional.

**The charitable-sounding strawman:** Your steelman sounds generous but subtly weakens the position — you've included the concessions the defender would make while omitting the strongest parts.
Detection: Would the position's defender recognize your steelman as stronger than their own statement of it?
Recovery: Return to Step 6 of Stage 1. Apply the test honestly.

**Empathy as condescension:** Stage 2 produces "I understand why someone with their background would believe this" — which is understanding the position as a symptom rather than as a view.
Recovery: The empathic steelman is not explaining why they're wrong in an understandable way. It's explaining why they're not obviously wrong from inside their own framework.

**Premature engagement:** You begin Stage 1 and your objections keep surfacing, pulling you toward rebuttal before the steelman is complete.
Recovery: Write the objections down. Return to them after both stages. They'll be sharper after the steelman anyway.

---

## Success Criteria

This worked when:
1. A thoughtful proponent would recognize Stage 1 as the strongest version of their argument
2. A genuine holder of the position would recognize Stage 2 as understanding why they hold it
3. You discovered something about the position you didn't know before
4. Your subsequent engagement addresses the real argument, not a weakened version

This failed when:
1. Your steelman is something the position's holder would find condescending or incomplete
2. You completed Stage 1 but skipped Stage 2 and called it done
3. You emerged with the same objections, unchanged, as when you started
4. The steelman was performed rather than genuinely constructed
