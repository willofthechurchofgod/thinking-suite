# INFER: Making Inferences Carefully and Clearly

## What This Skill Is

INFER is a configuration for making educated guesses, working from incomplete information, and reasoning from available evidence — while being explicit about the epistemic status of what you are inferring.

---

## The Configuration

**LENS:** epistemology:evidence-based-inference + logic:conditional-reasoning
**DEPTH:** 60-70% (deep enough to ground the inference in real evidence)
**LIGHT:** 80-100% (bright — you want to see hidden assumptions in your inference)

---

## How to Activate

**Step 1: State what you know**
Not: "They probably want X."
Better: "They said Y. I know Z about similar situations. This suggests they might want X."
Lead with evidence, not with inference.

**Step 2: Identify the gap**
What do you not know? What would you need to know to be certain?

**Step 3: Make the inference**
Given what you know and what you do not know, what is the best inference?
Make it as strong as the evidence warrants — no more.

**Step 4: Classify the inference**
- **Category II (Perceived True):** Strong evidence, reasonable inference
- **Category IVa (Empirically Open):** Evidence is mixed or weak, could go either way
- **Category IVb (Constitutively Open):** Question is not well-formed enough to answer

**Step 5: Calibrate your language**
Express the inference at the right confidence level for its actual category.
- Very solid: "X is the case" or "X almost certainly..."
- Moderately solid: "X is likely" or "The evidence suggests X"
- Weak: "X might be the case" or "It is possible that X, though evidence is mixed"
- Very weak: "The question is not clear enough to answer. We would need to clarify..."

---

## The Mechanics

### Phase 1: Evidence Inventory
List what you actually know:
- Direct statements ("They said X")
- Observed behavior ("They did X")
- Context ("In situation Y, people usually Z")
- Previous patterns ("They have done X before")

### Phase 2: Gap Identification
What would resolve the uncertainty?
What additional information would move this from Category II to Category I?

### Phase 3: The Inference Itself
Not the safest guess (that would be non-committal).
Not the most favorable guess (that would be wishful thinking).
The best guess given what you know.

### Phase 4: Strength Assessment
| Strength | Evidence | Confidence |
|---------|----------|-----------|
| Very solid (near Cat. I) | Multiple independent sources, few critical gaps | High |
| Moderately solid (Cat. II) | Some good evidence, some significant gaps | Medium |
| Weak (Cat. IVa) | Mixed evidence, major gaps, could go either way | Low |
| Very weak (Cat. IVb) | Ambiguous, question not well-formed | Do not infer yet |

### Phase 5: Language Calibration
Calibrate expression to match actual strength. Discipline yourself: say "might" when you mean "might."

## Flavors

**Behavioral Inference:** Infer intent or motivation from behavior. (60% depth, 90% light) — "Given these actions, what does this person probably care about?"

**Pattern Extrapolation:** Infer what will likely happen based on observed patterns. (70% depth, 80% light)

**Hidden Assumption Inference:** Infer what someone must believe to say/do what they said/did. (70% depth, 100% light)

**Gap-Filling Inference:** Infer the missing piece to make sense of what you do know. (60% depth, 80% light)

## Failure Modes

**Inferring without evidence:** You make a guess without grounding it in evidence. Recovery: list evidence before making inference. If there is none, use SPECULATE instead.

**Over-confidence in weak inferences:** You make a Category IVa inference and express it as Category II. Recovery: discipline yourself to express inferences at their actual strength.

**Confusing evidence with inference:** You state an inference as if it were evidence. Recovery: explicitly separate — "The evidence is X. The inference is Y."

**Missing the load-bearing gap:** Your inference depends on some missing information you did not notice. Recovery: ask "For this inference to be wrong, what would have to be false?"

---

## Success Criteria

INFER worked when:
1. You clearly separated evidence from inference
2. You identified the key gaps
3. You expressed the inference at its actual strength
4. You could explain why someone might reasonably disagree
5. You knew what additional information would change the inference

INFER failed when:
1. Evidence and inference were unclear
2. You expressed a weak inference as strong
3. You thought the inference was more certain than it was
